# AI for healthcare learners: study help without crossing the line

This page is for nurses, nursing students, and other healthcare learners who want to use free AI tools to **understand material**, not to make clinical decisions.

It is education only. It is not medical advice, clinical decision support, or a substitute for your course, your employer's policies, current protocols, or a qualified human.

> **The boundary:** AI can help you study a concept, organize your notes, or practise questions. It must not diagnose, treat, triage, calculate a medication dose, interpret a patient's results, or decide what care a person receives. Do not put protected health information (PHI) into a free consumer tool.

## The three rules

1. **Learning, not care.** Use AI to understand and rehearse. For patient care, use employer-approved systems, current policy, and the approved clinical process.
2. **No patient information.** No names, medical record numbers, dates of birth, images, addresses, rare details, or any combination that could identify a person. "De-identified" is harder than deleting a name.
3. **Verify before you rely.** Open the course text, clinical guideline, drug reference, or primary source. An AI answer without a source is a study prompt, not evidence.

If those rules sound strict, they are. A wrong answer in a study session costs you a quiz point. A wrong answer in care can hurt someone.

## Safe uses for study

| Goal | How AI can help | What you still do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Understand a concept | Ask for two explanations: simple and detailed | Check against your assigned reading |
| Prepare for a quiz | Turn de-identified notes into practice questions | Verify every answer and rationale |
| Compare sources | Use a source-grounded notebook with public documents | Open and read the cited passages |
| Organize a study plan | Ask for a weekly plan around your actual exam dates | Cut what does not fit your syllabus |
| Improve plain-language explanations | Draft a teaching explanation for a hypothetical patient | Follow your program's approved materials and communication standards |
| Practise math | Work through algebra or basic science steps | Treat medication calculation as a clinical skill with an approved method and double-check |

Good starter tools in the directory:

- [ChatGPT](tools/chat.md#tool-chatgpt), [Claude](tools/chat.md#tool-claude), or [Google Gemini](tools/chat.md#tool-google-gemini) for explanations and practice questions.
- [NotebookLM](tools/research.md#tool-notebooklm) for asking questions across public guidelines, open-access papers, or your own de-identified notes.
- [Wolfram Alpha](tools/education.md#tool-wolfram-alpha) for math and science practice, not for deciding a patient's dose.
- [Khanmigo](tools/education.md#tool-khanmigo) or [MagicSchool](tools/education.md#tool-magicschool) for learning and teaching practice, with review.

## Unsafe uses

Do not use a free AI tool to:

- diagnose a patient or suggest a treatment;
- assign a triage level or decide whether someone needs urgent care;
- calculate or check a medication dose for a real patient;
- interpret a patient's labs, imaging, monitor, or assessment;
- write or edit clinical documentation containing PHI;
- draft patient messages or discharge instructions without approved systems and review;
- summarize a real patient encounter;
- process patient photos, wounds, rashes, or scans;
- quiz yourself using real patient details, even if you think they are anonymous;
- make a decision because "the AI said so" when policy, a preceptor, or a clinician should decide.

If a question is about a real person's care, the tool is the wrong tool. Use the approved clinical workflow.

## Protect patient information

PHI is not just a name. It can include:

- dates, ages, locations, and facility names;
- medical record numbers, account numbers, and device identifiers;
- images, voice recordings, and unusual case details;
- combinations of ordinary facts that make one person identifiable.

Removing a name is not always enough. A rare condition, a small facility, a date, and a job title can identify someone. Follow your employer's or school's data rules, not your own judgment about what looks anonymous.

**Do not use a personal free account for your employer's data.** A personal account is not a workplace system, even if the same company makes an approved product. Your employer's contract, privacy terms, and access controls are part of the safety, not an optional extra.

Before uploading anything, ask:

1. Is this material public, or is it about a real person?
2. Has the person consented to this use?
3. Does my employer or school allow this exact tool and this exact data?
4. Could the data be reconstructed from what I am sharing?
5. Would I be comfortable explaining this upload to an auditor?

If any answer is no or unclear, do not upload it.

## A 45-minute study loop

Use one assistant and one public source. Keep the session small.

1. **Write the learning goal.** Example: "Explain how the kidneys regulate fluid balance at a beginner level." Not "Help me with renal."
2. **Ask for a first explanation.** Add: "Use plain language, define the terms, and mark anything that is a simplification."
3. **Teach it back.** Ask the assistant to quiz you, one question at a time. Answer in your own words.
4. **Verify with your course text or a guideline.** Check the definitions, numbers, and causal steps. If the AI and the source disagree, the source wins.
5. **Make five practice questions.** One definition, one comparison, one application, one common misconception, and one "what would change if…" question.
6. **Check the answer key.** Ask why plausible wrong answers are wrong. Verify every answer yourself.
7. **Write a one-page summary from memory.** Then reopen your notes and correct it. The correction is the studying.

Do not let the assistant write your assignment or clinical reflection. Follow your program's AI-use and disclosure rules.

## Explanations are not evidence

AI is good at producing a confident, tidy explanation. It can also invent a citation, blend two conditions, or state a number with no source.

Ask for:

- the underlying mechanism, not only a list of facts;
- the limits of the explanation and common misconceptions;
- a source you can open, not merely a citation-shaped sentence;
- uncertainty where the evidence is incomplete;
- a comparison with the assigned reading.

Then open the source. If a citation leads to a real paper, check whether the paper supports the claim. A real-looking reference can still be irrelevant or misrepresented.

## Medication calculations are clinical work

A calculator can help you learn a formula. It cannot take responsibility for a dose. Medication calculation for a real patient belongs to the approved clinical process, with the required double-check and a qualified human.

For study, use your program's approved method and show your work. For care, follow policy. Do not ask a chatbot for a dose or use its arithmetic as a second checker.

## AI at work

Healthcare workplaces have rules for a reason. If you are using AI at work:

- use only employer-approved tools and accounts;
- follow the policy for clinical documentation and patient messages;
- do not connect a personal AI assistant to work email, records, or a browser profile;
- do not upload PHI to an MCP server, agent, or "private" local app without approval; see [Agents and MCP](agents-and-mcp.md);
- do not let an agent send, schedule, order, or document anything without the approved review process;
- ask before recording a meeting, lecture, handoff, or conversation;
- report a suspected data exposure through the proper channel.

"Local" does not automatically make regulated data acceptable. If the file is not allowed on your laptop, a private model beside it does not change the rule.

## For educators and preceptors

When teaching with AI:

1. Set a clear AI-use and disclosure policy for the course or unit.
2. Use invented, public, or fully de-identified material in demonstrations.
3. Show students a confident wrong answer and model the verification step.
4. Never upload student records, evaluations, or placement details to a free tool.
5. Check accessibility and provide an alternative for students who cannot or do not want to use AI.
6. Keep clinical judgment, professional communication, and supervised practice in human hands.

Students should not be put in a position where using an unapproved tool is the only way to complete a task.

## A privacy checklist

Before any study upload:

- [ ] The material is public or invented.
- [ ] No patient, classmate, colleague, or employee is identifiable.
- [ ] The tool is allowed by my school or employer.
- [ ] I understand what the provider may do with uploads.
- [ ] No account password, API key, or credential is in the material.
- [ ] I have a way to check the answer without the AI.

If you cannot tick every box, keep the material out.

## If something goes wrong

If you think PHI was entered into an unapproved tool:

1. Stop using the tool for that material.
2. Do not delete evidence or try to hide the mistake.
3. Report it promptly through your employer's or school's privacy/security incident process.
4. Follow their instructions for containment and notification.
5. Change any exposed credentials if applicable.

The goal is to protect people, not to protect your pride. Prompt reporting is part of professional practice.
