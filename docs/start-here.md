# Start here

You do not need 165 AI tools. You need the right two for the job in front of you.

This page turns the directory into short paths for real situations. Each path recommends a small starting set, explains the first useful step, and names the catch so you can avoid it. Every link goes to the full listing with limits and an official link.

> **The two-tool rule:** for the next week, pick one tool for thinking and one tool for making. Learn those two. Ignore the rest. When the job changes, you can change the pair.

## A note about every path

- Free plans are snapshots from **October 22, 2026**. Check the provider's current terms.
- Nothing here is medical, legal, financial, or clinical advice.
- Never put confidential information into a free tool: patient details, client files, student records, passwords, API keys, unpublished manuscripts, identity documents, or financial account data.
- Free access is not the same as a commercial licence. If you plan to publish or sell the output, read the plan and model terms.
- A tool that saves you time is useful. A tool that adds cleanup work is not. You are allowed to stop.

## Brand new to AI

**Start with:** [ChatGPT](tools/chat.md#tool-chatgpt) · [Google Gemini](tools/chat.md#tool-google-gemini) · [Claude](tools/chat.md#tool-claude)

Pick one general assistant. Not all three. They are all capable of explaining a topic, organizing notes, drafting a first pass, and helping you ask better questions.

**A calm first session (20 minutes):**

1. Open one assistant and ask it to explain something you already know, such as "Explain what a normal week at my job looks like, in beginner terms." Check whether the explanation sounds like you.
2. Ask follow-up questions in plain language. "That part about billing confused me. Say it again without jargon." If the tool is condescending, you can tell it to change tone.
3. Try one real, low-stakes task: outline a grocery list, plan a short trip, or explain a paragraph you found confusing.
4. Ask for the source when the answer contains a fact, number, quote, or medical claim. Then check it yourself.

**Add one maker:** [Canva](tools/images.md#tool-canva) for a simple design, or [Grammarly](tools/writing.md#tool-grammarly) for a message you already wrote. Keep the assistant as your thinking tool.

**The catch:** assistants can sound certain when they are wrong. Treat every answer as a draft from a helpful person who has not checked the source. Do not paste private or unpublished work into a free account without reading the privacy terms.

**Skip for now:** agents, automation chains, API keys, and five-tool "stacks." They will still be here when you have a job for them.

## Authors, publishers, and KDP

**Start with:** [Grammarly](tools/writing.md#tool-grammarly) · [LanguageTool](tools/writing.md#tool-languagetool) · [QuillBot](tools/writing.md#tool-quillbot) · [Canva](tools/images.md#tool-canva) · [Ideogram](tools/images.md#tool-ideogram) · [NotebookLM](tools/research.md#tool-notebooklm)

AI is good at first passes, variants, editing questions, and simple design exploration. It is not a ghostwriter you can trust without editing, a fact-checker, or a copyright lawyer.

**A practical author workflow:**

1. **Keep your own draft untouched.** Save a copy before any AI edit. Your voice is the asset.
2. **Use editing tools as questions, not commands.** Run one chapter through Grammarly or LanguageTool for mechanics. Use QuillBot to see an alternative phrasing when a sentence resists you, then rewrite it yourself.
3. **Research with sources open.** NotebookLM can help you compare public notes, open-access papers, or research you have permission to upload. Open every cited source before you rely on it. The same caution applies to [Semantic Scholar](tools/research.md#tool-semantic-scholar) and [Elicit](tools/research.md#tool-elicit).
4. **Design covers as drafts.** Canva and Ideogram can explore mood, color, and composition. Do not trust small AI-rendered text; set the final title and spine copy yourself. Check the licence for every generated element. Keep records of the model and plan you used.
5. **Clean audio, not voices.** [Adobe Podcast](tools/audio.md#tool-adobe-podcast) can improve a recording you made yourself. Voice cloning with [ElevenLabs](tools/audio.md#tool-elevenlabs) is an experiment, not a shortcut around consent or audiobook contractual terms.

**Before you publish:**

- Check the current plan's commercial-use and attribution terms, plus the model's licence.
- Follow KDP and other marketplace rules for AI-generated content and disclosure, including images and translations.
- Do not use a real person's name, face, or trademark as a character or cover element without permission.
- Verify names, dates, quotes, citations, and legal or medical claims yourself.
- Keep a version history of human drafts and final files. Your edit trail protects you.

**The catch:** free design and writing tools can claim broad rights to process your input, and free plans can change. Do not upload a full unpublished manuscript to a service whose privacy policy you have not read. A chapter sample is enough to evaluate the editing experience.

## Small business and solo work

**Start with:** [ChatGPT](tools/chat.md#tool-chatgpt) or [Google Gemini](tools/chat.md#tool-google-gemini) · [Canva](tools/images.md#tool-canva) · [Zapier](tools/productivity.md#tool-zapier) or [Make](tools/productivity.md#tool-make) · [Scribe](tools/productivity.md#tool-scribe) · [Gamma](tools/productivity.md#tool-gamma)

The useful small-business wins are unglamorous: a faster first draft, a repeatable process note, a clean social post, a meeting summary, a simple presentation. Start there before you automate your entire back office.

**Four jobs that are worth testing:**

1. **Customer-facing words.** Draft a FAQ answer or service description, then edit it for accuracy and tone. Never let AI invent prices, policies, guarantees, or credentials.
2. **Simple visuals.** Use Canva templates for a consistent post format. Check font and image licences, and keep a human review step before anything goes public.
3. **Process notes.** Use Scribe to document a task once, then improve the written steps. This is often more valuable than a complicated workflow.
4. **Meeting follow-up.** With everyone's consent, try [Fathom](tools/productivity.md#tool-fathom), [Granola](tools/productivity.md#tool-granola), or [Otter.ai](tools/audio.md#tool-otter-ai). Never record a client, patient, or employee conversation without the required permission. Summaries need review before they become a record.

**Automation caution:** Zapier, Make, n8n, and [Activepieces](tools/productivity.md#tool-activepieces) can move business data across services. Map the process first, use test data, grant the narrowest permissions, and keep a way to stop the workflow. An automation between the wrong two fields can send private information to the wrong place very quickly.

**Bookkeeping note:** this guide does not recommend a free AI bookkeeping tool. Keep financial records in a system you understand, back up your data, and use a qualified professional for tax or accounting decisions.

**The catch:** "free" workflow builders count tasks, not value. A workflow that uses 100 tasks to save ten minutes may cost more than it returns once you factor in maintenance and risk.

## Healthcare workers and students

> **Health boundary:** use these tools to **study and understand concepts**, never to diagnose, treat, triage, calculate medication doses, or make care decisions. This guide is not medical advice, clinical decision support, or a HIPAA-compliant system. Follow your employer's or school's policy. Do not put patient information or protected health information (PHI) into a free consumer account.

**Start with:** [ChatGPT](tools/chat.md#tool-chatgpt) or [Claude](tools/chat.md#tool-claude) · [NotebookLM](tools/research.md#tool-notebooklm) · [Khanmigo](tools/education.md#tool-khanmigo) or [Wolfram Alpha](tools/education.md#tool-wolfram-alpha) for practice outside clinical decisions

This path is short on purpose. The guide deliberately does not include clinical decision-support tools. For patient care, use employer-approved systems, current protocols, and a qualified human. AI can help you become a better learner; it cannot carry clinical responsibility.

**Safe study uses:**

- Ask an assistant to explain a physiology concept in two levels, then check it against your course text. Ask, "What would a common misconception be here?"
- Turn your own lecture notes, with all names and identifiers removed, into practice questions. Verify every answer against a trusted source.
- Use NotebookLM with public guidelines, open-access papers, or your own de-identified study notes to compare sources. It can point you to passages; it cannot guarantee that a summary is clinically complete.
- Practice algebra or basic science reasoning with Wolfram Alpha or an education tool, while recognizing that **medication dose calculation is a clinical task** and must follow your program's and employer's approved method and double-check process.

**Do not:**

- Paste patient notes, names, MRNs, dates of birth, images, or any other PHI into a free tool.
- Ask a chatbot for a diagnosis, treatment plan, dose, triage level, or interpretation of a patient's results.
- Use a generated summary as the sole source for a clinical claim, exam answer, or citation.
- Upload a classmate's, patient's, or colleague's information to test a tool.
- Use personal free accounts for work involving your employer's data.

**The catch:** even a "private" chat can be logged, reviewed, or used to improve a model. De-identification is harder than deleting a name. If you cannot explain where the data goes and who can see it, do not upload it.

## Teachers and students

**Start with:** [Khanmigo](tools/education.md#tool-khanmigo) · [MagicSchool](tools/education.md#tool-magicschool) · [Quizlet](tools/education.md#tool-quizlet) · [Diffit](tools/education.md#tool-diffit) · [Teachable Machine](tools/education.md#tool-teachable-machine)

Free educator access is often more generous than student access, and it can change by region or verification status. Check the current terms for your role.

**For teachers:**

1. Use MagicSchool or Khanmigo to draft a lesson outline, discussion questions, or a differentiated reading — then review it for accuracy, age-appropriateness, and your actual curriculum.
2. Use Diffit to explore reading-level adaptations. A generated version is a starting draft; a teacher still decides what a student needs.
3. Try Quizlet or Kahoot for practice sets the class will enjoy, but check every answer key before students see it.
4. Teach the limits out loud. Show students a confident wrong answer, then model how to verify it with a primary source. That lesson outlasts any tool.
5. Keep student data out of free accounts unless your district has approved the service and its data terms.

**For students:**

- Use AI to ask better questions, generate practice, and explain a sticking point. Do not submit work you did not do.
- Follow your school's AI-use and disclosure rules. When unsure, ask before, not after.
- Verify facts, citations, calculations, and code. A tutor that never says "I don't know" is not a reliable tutor.
- Protect classmates: no photos, names, grades, or private messages in a prompt.

**The catch:** education tools can generate plausible but wrong questions, answers, and reading levels. They also may not be accessible to every learner. Keep a human teacher, an alternative format, and a backup plan in the loop.

## Accessibility and independent living

> These tools can support access to information. They are **not** a replacement for professional assessment, mobility training, captioning services, or an emergency plan. Features and device support vary. Try a tool in the conditions where you actually need it, before you need it, and keep a backup method for critical tasks.

**Start with:** [Seeing AI](tools/accessibility.md#tool-seeing-ai) · [Be My Eyes](tools/accessibility.md#tool-be-my-eyes) · [Live Transcribe](tools/accessibility.md#tool-live-transcribe)

**A practical test plan:**

1. Try one task you do often — reading a label, identifying a bill, following a short conversation, captioning a video — with the tool and with your current method.
2. Test in real conditions: dim light, background noise, poor signal, a rushed moment. A demo that works at a quiet desk may not work in a clinic or on a bus.
3. Learn where the output fails. Does it misread small print? Merge speakers? Lag behind? Miss unusual names?
4. Set up a second method for emergencies and essential information. That could be a human helper, a dedicated accessibility service, a printed backup, or a different device.
5. Review privacy before pointing a camera or microphone at people, documents, or screens. Ask for consent where it applies.

**Other tools to explore:** [Google Lookout](tools/accessibility.md#tool-google-lookout), [SuperSense](tools/accessibility.md#tool-supersense), [Google Voice Access](tools/accessibility.md#tool-google-voice-access), [Microsoft Translator](tools/accessibility.md#tool-microsoft-translator), [Windows Live Captions](tools/accessibility.md#tool-windows-live-captions), [Apple Live Captions](tools/accessibility.md#tool-apple-live-captions), and [Chrome Live Caption](tools/accessibility.md#tool-chrome-live-caption).

**The catch:** free accessibility features can be discontinued, limited by device or region, or blocked by workplace IT policy. A free tool is not a reasonable accommodation plan by itself. Keep the human and professional services you rely on, and test updates before they become essential.

## Low-energy and ADHD-friendly work

AI will not fix executive function, and it should not become another dashboard to maintain. The goal here is smaller: **lower the number of open loops, lower the number of decisions, and make the first step obvious.** This is practical workflow advice, not medical advice.

**Use the two-tool rule, then lower it.** One tool for thinking, one for making. Some weeks, one tool is enough.

**A 10-minute reset:**

1. Brain-dump into your phone's voice-memo app or one chat thread. Messy is fine. "Everything in my head right now" is a valid prompt.
2. Ask one assistant: "Group this into three next actions, smallest first. Do not add new projects." with [ChatGPT](tools/chat.md#tool-chatgpt) or [Gemini](tools/chat.md#tool-google-gemini).
3. Choose one action that takes 15 minutes or less. Put everything else out of sight.
4. Use a timer. When it ends, stop or choose deliberately to continue. Stopping on purpose is allowed.
5. If a tool added cleanup, decisions, or notifications, drop it. That is a successful experiment, not a failure.

**Low-friction picks:**

- [Gamma](tools/productivity.md#tool-gamma) to get a rough presentation structure instead of staring at a blank deck.
- [Canva](tools/images.md#tool-canva) templates when "make it look normal" is the real blocker.
- [Scribe](tools/productivity.md#tool-scribe) to write a process down once so future-you does not have to remember every step.
- [Otter.ai](tools/audio.md#tool-otter-ai) or [Fathom](tools/productivity.md#tool-fathom) for a meeting recap with consent and review, not a second full-time inbox.
- [NotebookLM](tools/research.md#tool-notebooklm) to keep a project's sources in one place instead of forty abandoned tabs.

**Design rules for this path:**

- One browser tab, one tool, one task.
- Turn off notifications and autoplay. Do not accept a daily streak as a debt.
- Keep prompts somewhere you will find them; reuse beats reinvention.
- Use the guide's category filter to reduce the visible list, not to plan a bigger one.
- Stop rules are part of the workflow: "When the timer ends," "when I have a rough draft," "when the next step needs a person."

**The catch:** transcript tools produce a new document that someone still has to review. Automation can create invisible failures. Check the output before it becomes a decision, a reminder, or a client record.

If your main concern is keeping conversations and files on hardware you control, look at [Ollama](tools/chat.md#tool-ollama), [LM Studio](tools/chat.md#tool-lm-studio), [Jan](tools/chat.md#tool-jan), [GPT4All](tools/chat.md#tool-gpt4all), [Open WebUI](tools/chat.md#tool-open-webui), and [LibreChat](tools/chat.md#tool-librechat). ## Fun, games, and side quests

Learning sticks better when the project is fun. These are legitimate uses, not guilty pleasures.

**Try:**

- **Character portraits and table handouts:** [Ideogram](tools/images.md#tool-ideogram) or [Leonardo AI](tools/images.md#tool-leonardo-ai) for a draft portrait; [Canva](tools/images.md#tool-canva) to add legible text and layout. Keep prompts and licenses; avoid using another person's likeness without permission.
- **A theme song for your project:** [Suno](tools/audio.md#tool-suno) or [Udio](tools/audio.md#tool-udio) — as an experiment first. Check commercial-use and streaming rules before publishing.
- **A tiny trailer or music-video experiment:** [Pika](tools/video.md#tool-pika), [Runway](tools/video.md#tool-runway), or [Wan Video](tools/video.md#tool-wan-video) if you can run it.
- **FFXIV lore refreshers:** ask [ChatGPT](tools/chat.md#tool-chatgpt) or [Claude](tools/chat.md#tool-claude) for a recap with a spoiler warning, then check a wiki for exact names and quests. Do not automate gameplay or share account credentials with any tool.
- **Chess study:** ask an assistant to explain a position's ideas in plain English or to quiz you on tactics motifs. Use a real board or chess app to verify the position; language models can misread a board.
- **A weird little chatbot for a story:** [Character.AI](tools/chat.md#tool-character-ai) is fun for roleplay. It is not a person, a therapist, or a reliable canon keeper.

**The catch:** generated characters can look or sound like real people, and fan works have their own legal and platform rules. Use your own characters, credit inspiration, and do not publish someone's cloned voice or face. Keep game account credentials out of prompts.

## A calm first week

| Day | Small action |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Write down one job you would genuinely like to make easier. |
| 2 | Open one assistant. Try that job with invented or public material. |
| 3 | Read the limits and privacy terms of that one tool. |
| 4 | Add one maker tool from a different category, and use it for one small output. |
| 5 | Review: did either tool save time or improve the result? Keep or drop without guilt. |
| 6 | Write down your two keepers and one rule for private data. |
| 7 | Rest. The tools will still be here. |

## When to stop

Stop using a tool when:

- you cannot explain what it does with your data;
- the output needs more checking than doing the task yourself;
- it pressures you with streaks, scarcity, or daily logins;
- the free plan disappears and you do not actually need the paid one;
- it asks for information that is not required for the job;
- it is replacing a human relationship, a clinical judgment, or a professional service.

The best free tool is the smallest one that gets the job done and lets you get back to your life.
