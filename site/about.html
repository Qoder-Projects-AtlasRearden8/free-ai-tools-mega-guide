01

Why the guide exists

AI is already buried in tools people use every day, but “free” is often doing a lot of quiet work. A plan may be free only for teachers. An image tool may be free to look at but not to sell. A chat app may charge you in data rather than dollars. The useful question is not “Is there a free plan?” It is “What can I actually do with this without a surprise?”

This directory answers that question in the same place for every tool: what is free, what sign-up asks for, where the limits show up, and what the tool is best at. It includes mainstream assistants, local and open-source software, creative tools, study helpers, and accessibility features. It does not include a tool just because it has an impressive demo.

The rule behind every listing

If the free path is not genuinely useful for at least one real task, it does not belong in the guide. “Free” can mean a permanent free tier, a free core, open-source software you run yourself, or a self-hosted tool. It should never mean “free after you give us a card and hope you remember to cancel.”

The guide follows the plain-English, step-by-step teaching style behind The Massive AI Manual and dylanasdale.github.io (opens in a new tab). No jargon gates. No condescension. A clear next step on every page.

02

How tools are chosen

Every candidate has to clear the same short list:

  • A real no-cost path. A free plan, free core, open-source project, or self-hosted option that a beginner can reach without a mandatory purchase.
  • A clear use case. The tool should do a recognisable job, not merely exist as a wrapper around another model.
  • An official link. No affiliate redirect, clone store, or scraped download page.
  • Clear sign-up notes. Every entry names the account, install, or verification step and whether a card is expected for the free path.
  • Honest limits. Every entry states the practical catch, including watermarks, credit caps, public projects, hardware needs, and paid gates.
  • A reasonable safety posture. We avoid tools whose main free offer depends on deceptive data collection or unlawful use, and we flag privacy and consent issues where they matter.

We deliberately leave out paid-only products, expired trials that require a card, repackaged copies of another company's model, and services that present themselves as clinical decision tools. Inclusion is not an endorsement or a security audit.

Verification in this edition is editorial and link-based. We reviewed providers' public offer and sign-up pages and checked official links, but we did not create paid accounts or run a controlled performance benchmark for every tool. Account checks, regional availability, and free limits can change by country, account age, device, and current demand. “No card expected” is a plain-language note, not a guarantee: stop if a card field appears. When in doubt, the provider's own terms win.

03

What “last verified” means

Each tool record carries a last_verified date. This edition uses October 22, 2026 for all entries because the public offers were reviewed together for release. The date is a point-in-time snapshot, not a guarantee that the offer will still exist tomorrow.

“Verified” here means the entry was compared with the provider's public information and its official link was checked. It does not mean we tested every feature, endorsed the company, audited its security, or confirmed that the limit fits your situation.

Free plans are moving targets. Model names get renamed, credits get reduced, regions change, and companies get acquired. Before you upload work, start a business process, or recommend a tool to a class, open its current pricing page and terms. If the official page disagrees with this guide, trust the official page.

Update habit

When a tool changes, correct the category JSON, update the date on the affected record, and rebuild the guide. A directory that hides stale claims is less useful than one that says exactly when it was checked.

04

How access and sign-up notes work

Each card carries one access label and one sign-up note. The label describes how the free path works, not how good the tool is.

Free tier

A no-cost plan with caps, feature gates, queues, or usage credits. It should not require a card unless the entry says otherwise.

Free

Free to use at the core. Optional upgrades or platform purchases may still exist.

Open source

Source code is available and you can run it yourself. The software may be free; the computer, storage, electricity, and model downloads are not.

Self-hosted

The software can run on your machine or server under the provider's source-available terms. You supply setup, maintenance, and model access.

Sign-up notes state whether a free account, app install, or verification is needed and whether a card is expected. They are a convenience, not a contract. If an offer you checked as free now asks for payment details, region-locked ID, or a paid trial, leave the flow and recheck the provider's pricing page.

A locally run tool can be a better privacy choice than a cloud service, but local does not mean risk-free. Model files can be untrusted, extensions can overreach, and local logs can still expose sensitive information. Treat local tools as tools, not magic erasers.

05

Privacy and account safety

Never put patient information, client records, passwords, API keys, Social Security numbers, financial account data, private addresses, or unpublished manuscripts into a free AI tool unless you have clear permission and a genuine data agreement. Free often means broad service rights, limited retention controls, and human review for quality. Those are not the conditions for confidential data.

  • Use a unique password and multi-factor authentication. Do not reuse your email or banking password just because the AI tool is fun.
  • Check the renewal screen. If a card is required, note the trial end date and cancel immediately if you are not ready to pay.
  • Read what “private” means. A private chat can still be logged, reviewed, or used to improve a service. Read the policy rather than assuming.
  • Do not upload API keys. A key is a password for a paid service. Keep it out of chats, screenshots, repositories, and prompts.
  • Export before leaving. Free accounts can disappear. Keep your own copies of documents and outputs you care about.

This website does not run analytics, advertising pixels, accounts, or third-party scripts. The directory loads a local JSON file. Clicking an official link takes you to a provider that has its own privacy policy and data practices.

06

Rights, licenses, and health limits

Free access is not the same as a commercial licence. A plan can let you generate an image but not sell it, let you make a song but not monetise it, or let you use a model for a project while requiring attribution. Read the plan's terms and the model's licence. Keep records of what tool, model, and plan you used for anything you publish.

  • Credit others' work. AI does not erase copyright, trademarks, privacy rights, or likeness rights.
  • Follow publishing rules. Marketplaces and publishers have their own AI-content and disclosure policies. Check them before publishing a book, template, or client deliverable.
  • Disclose synthetic media when required. Do not present generated faces, voices, or events as real footage. Never clone a voice or face without permission.
  • Keep a human in the loop. Check facts, citations, calculations, code, accessibility, and legal or medical claims. A confident answer is not evidence.

Health boundary

This guide is educational. It is not medical advice, clinical decision support, or a HIPAA-compliant system. Do not use these tools to diagnose, treat, triage, calculate medication doses, or make care decisions. Do not paste protected health information into a free consumer account. Healthcare workers should follow their employer's policy and use approved systems for anything involving a patient.

The tools in the accessibility category can support access to information, but they are not a replacement for professional assessment, mobility training, captioning services, or emergency planning. Keep a backup method for critical tasks.

07

A calmer way to try tools

You do not need to test everything in this directory. A good starter loop is small:

  1. Choose one job. “Summarise a public report” is a job. “Use AI more” is not.
  2. Pick one tool. Use the category filters and a start-here pick.
  3. Read the catch before signing up. Free tier, limits, and official link are on every card.
  4. Try a low-stakes input. Test with public or invented material, not patient data or a client's confidential file.
  5. Ask: did this save real time or add useful quality? If not, leave it. A smaller toolbox is easier to use.

For readers who find big tool lists exhausting: pick two tools for a week, not twenty. One assistant for questions and one maker for the thing you actually produce. Write down the two jobs on a sticky note. Ignore the rest until the note changes.

08

Who this is for

Curious beginners

Start with a general assistant, then try one creative or practical tool. Plain explanations, no assumed vocabulary.

Authors and publishers

Use writing and image tools for drafts, covers, and promotion, while keeping rights, citations, and human editing in view.

Small businesses

Automation, meeting notes, design, and simple video can cut busywork. Test with public data and keep a person responsible for final output.

Healthcare learners

Use AI to study concepts and make learning aids, never for clinical decisions. Keep patient information out of free tools.

Teachers and students

Free educator plans are included where they exist. Check school policy, disclose AI use as required, and verify generated questions and answers.

Accessibility users

Try supported features on your own device before trusting them in an emergency. Keep a backup method for essential information.

Low-energy days

Use filters to reduce choices, not expand them. Pick the smallest useful tool and stop when the job is done.

Fun and side quests

Images, characters, music, games, and experiments count. Learning is easier when the first project is something you actually enjoy.

09

Scope and corrections

This edition covers ten categories: chat and general assistants, writing and editing, images and design, video and animation, audio and voice, coding and development, research and knowledge, productivity and automation, education and learning, and accessibility. Tools appear once, in their strongest category, even when they could fit several places.

The guide is intentionally provider-neutral. No company paid to appear. No link is an affiliate link. A tool being listed does not mean its terms are fair, its model is accurate, or its data practices fit your work. It means the free path is substantial enough to be worth explaining.

Corrections should be made at the source, not patched into generated pages. Update data/categories/*.json with the current offer and a truthful verification date, then run npm test and rebuild. The Markdown pages and site/data/tools.json are generated from that data.
